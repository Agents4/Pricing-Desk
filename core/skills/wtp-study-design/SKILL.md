# Willingness-to-Pay Study Design

Plan a pricing study that can change a decision, and analyze the results honestly. Two modes: **design** (before fieldwork) and **analyze** (after data arrives). Ask which one is needed if unclear.

## Design mode

### Inputs to collect first

1. **The pricing decision** the study must inform (launch price, tier boundaries, bundle, new value metric, price increase).
2. **Product concept**: what it does, who uses it, how it is delivered, current alternatives.
3. **Target segments** and who in each buys or influences the decision (developer, product owner, procurement, agency owner).
4. **Hypotheses** about value drivers, price levels, and value metrics.
5. **Constraints**: budget, timeline, access to customers, anything customers must not be asked.

### Steps

1. **State the decision and the hypotheses** in two or three sentences each. If the study will not change the decision, say so and propose a cheaper method.
2. **Choose the method** and explain why: customer interviews and value-driver quantification (early, enterprise, small samples); Van Westendorp or Gabor-Granger (price range for a defined product); conjoint or max-diff (packaging, feature, and tier trade-offs, needs enough respondents); analysis of past deals and win-loss (cheap, grounded in behavior). Prefer a combination when samples are small, which is usual in B2B travel technology.
3. **Plan the sample**: segments, quotas, how to recruit, and the minimum useful size per cell. State what can and cannot be concluded at that size.
4. **Draft the instrument**: interview guide or questionnaire with neutral wording, no leading price anchors, screening questions, and the value-metric questions (what unit should they pay for: calls, bookings, seats, records).
5. **Plan the analysis**: what charts and tests, and what result would change the recommendation.
6. **Flag risks**: stated versus actual willingness to pay, buyer-user gaps, confidentiality, competition-law concerns when asking customers about competitors, and fatigue from heavy customer contact.

### Output (design)

Study brief (one page), instrument, sample plan, analysis plan, timeline, and a decision rule: "if X, recommend Y."

## Analyze mode

### Inputs

Raw responses or interview notes, the instrument, the sample plan, and current price book.

### Steps

1. **Check the data** with code: sample sizes by segment, completion, straight-lining, outliers, and bias risks. Report them first.
2. **Compute results with code**: for Van Westendorp, the four price curves and the acceptable range; for Gabor-Granger, the demand curve and revenue-maximizing point; for conjoint, part-worths and simulated shares; for interviews, coded themes with counts and quotes kept short.
3. **Segment the results** and show where segments disagree.
4. **Translate to recommendations**: price range and a recommended starting point, value metric, tier or bundle structure, and fences. State confidence and what would raise it.
5. **List limits**: sample size, sampling bias, hypothetical bias, and what to test next in market.

### Output (analyze)

Findings in order: the answer to the decision, supporting figures, segment differences, recommended price and packaging, confidence and limits, and a proposed in-market test.

## Rules

- Compute all statistics with code and show sample sizes next to every figure.
- Never present a stated willingness to pay as a demand forecast.
- Do not fabricate respondent data or benchmark figures; if no benchmark is supplied, do not cite one.
- Do not contact customers; the research analyst fields the study and approves the findings.
- Keep respondent identities and customer-specific answers confidential in any summary.
